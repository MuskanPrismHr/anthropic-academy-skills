## Purpose

pK-12 classrooms add a constraint most AI-fluency advice skips past: nearly everything touches minors, whose data is protected by law and whose relationship to a teacher is the actual mechanism of learning. This skill applies the 4D framework (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) to that reality, and closes with how to carry it to colleagues as a trainer rather than just practice it solo.

## The Description-Discernment loop for instructional materials

Effective Description here means giving AI the specific texture of a real classroom, not a generic request: the standard or learning target the material supports, the actual range of learners in the room, at least one specific accommodation need, and what students will do with the material once it exists. A prompt built from a generic request produces a generic worksheet; a prompt built from "my students read below grade level and need key vocabulary defined in context" produces something usable.

Discernment on the result means running a concrete checklist before it goes anywhere near a student: is the content accurate for the subject area, did it land at the right level with key vocabulary introduced and supported, does it actually support what students will do with it and where the lesson needs to go, and would anything land badly with these specific students. When something's off, write a concrete revision instruction ("shorten the steps in the center directions and add a picture cue for each one") rather than "make it better" - vague feedback produces another vague draft. Uploading past unit plans, rubrics, or strong examples helps AI match an educator's actual voice and rigor rather than defaulting to generic instructional-materials tone.

## The Delegation-Diligence loop for student data

This is the loop with legal weight attached: data protections like FERPA apply to AI tools exactly as they apply to any other system, which means only district-approved tools are appropriate for anything containing student information, full stop. Delegation here means deciding upfront which parts of a data task (attendance patterns, performance summaries, survey results) are safe to hand to AI and which must stay with the educator, before any data gets shared. Diligence means validating results before trusting them and being transparent about AI's role in producing them. Because policy doesn't cover every situation, responsible use in practice means navigating gray areas with pedagogical judgment, not just rule-following - and revisiting that judgment as students, tools, and context change, not settling it once.

## Naming the ethical tensions that actually apply to your classroom

Rather than treating "use AI ethically" as a slogan, name the one or two tensions that are genuinely live for you right now: dependency (when might relying on AI replace thinking students need to do themselves), de-socialization (when could AI displace human interaction or relationship-building), bias (when might AI reinforce narrow perspectives or existing inequities), student agency (who actually controls how AI gets used - student, educator, or system), equity of access (who benefits from AI and who gets excluded by access or resources), and environmental impact (how compute and energy use factor into the decision). Naming a tension is only useful if it becomes a commitment: what you won't delegate because of it, what "useful" means for you without worsening it, and what you'll do when you're unsure (pause, consult a colleague, review district guidance, pilot cautiously). This is meant to be revisited, not finalized once.

## Building a personal AI value document

A generic "AI ethics best practices" document doesn't hold anyone to anything; a personal one does. Turn a context document (built early, capturing values and classroom reality) plus the ethical commitments above into a short document with three sections: Intentions, Principles, and Boundaries - the 2-3 rules you want to hold yourself to, and the one kind of work you'll always do yourself regardless of how good AI gets at it. The document only works if it's actually yours: read the AI-drafted version critically, fix what doesn't sound like you, add what's missing, cut what isn't true. AI is not the pedagogy - the document's job is to keep the educator, not the tool, in the driver's seat, and it stays alive by being revisited as tools and context change, not filed away.

## Running the full workflow end to end

Once each D has been practiced separately, the real skill is moving between them fluidly on one real task: decide what's worth handing off and what stays yours (Delegation), frame the request with real context (Description), evaluate what comes back against subject expertise and classroom knowledge (Discernment), and take full ownership of the final result, including being transparent about how it was produced where that matters (Diligence). The habit worth building is owning the process, not just the output - the work isn't "done" the moment AI finishes, and the mark of fluency is that the four Ds stop feeling like a checklist and start feeling like how the work simply gets done.

## Facilitating for colleagues

The decisions being made about AI in education right now will shape classrooms for years, and educators need to be in that room - no system replicates the relational work at the center of teaching, which is exactly why educator judgment has to stay central as adoption spreads. Bringing colleagues along works best with a ready-to-run structure rather than improvising each time: a slide deck to present, a facilitator guide with the talking points and activity setup for each slide (built in as toggleable speaker notes), and a learner workbook so participants practice the 4Ds themselves rather than just watching a demonstration. Anyone facilitating this for the first time should have already built their own fluency through the practice above - the kit assumes a foundation, it doesn't build one from scratch.

## Quick reference

- Creating instructional materials with AI → give real classroom context (standard, learner range, specific accommodation, intended use) up front, then check accuracy, reading level, and fit before it reaches a student.
- Feeding AI anything with student names, grades, attendance, or performance data → district-approved tools only; this is a FERPA question, not a style preference.
- Facing an AI-use situation policy doesn't clearly cover → that's a judgment call, not a rule lookup; name which ethical tension is actually at stake and decide deliberately.
- Feeling like "be ethical about AI" is too vague to act on → turn it into a personal value document (Intentions/Principles/Boundaries) built from your own context, not a generic template.
- A revision to AI-generated material isn't landing → write a concrete instruction naming the exact fix, not "make it better."
- Training colleagues on AI fluency → use a structured kit (deck, facilitator guide, participant workbook) rather than improvising, and only after you've built your own fluency first.
