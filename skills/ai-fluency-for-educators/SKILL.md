## Purpose

Condensed reference to Anthropic Academy's "AI Fluency for Educators" course, which applies the general AI Fluency Framework (4Ds) specifically to teaching. Pairs with `ai-fluency-framework` (the general foundations course) — this skill is the education-specific application layer: building a teaching context document, then using it to design courses and build the materials students actually use. Pull the relevant section rather than repeating the whole thing.

## Why context-building comes first

A generic AI assistant, asked to help with teaching, gives generic answers. The course's foundational move is building a **reusable teaching context document** before doing any actual course-design or materials work — a structured summary of who you are as an educator that you can drop into any future AI conversation to skip re-explaining yourself every time.

The framing throughout is **augmentation, not automation**: AI should add to your teaching judgment, not substitute for it. That distinction matters more in education than almost any other domain, since the whole point is developing *student* thinking, not just producing an artifact quickly.

### Building the teaching context document

Before opening an AI conversation, get clear with yourself first:
- What are your core teaching values and philosophy?
- What constraints do you face (institutional, technological, time)?
- Who are your students, typically (background, challenges, goals)?
- What teaching methods or learning experiences do you value most, and why?

Then have an actual back-and-forth conversation with an AI assistant to build the document — either invite it to interview you, or start sharing information and ask what else would help. Cover: subject area and specific courses, typical student challenges, institutional context, teaching philosophy and preferred methods, common pain points, and your goals for using AI at all. Be conversational rather than dumping everything at once, encourage follow-up questions, and stay mindful of student privacy when sharing examples. End by asking the AI to synthesize the conversation into a structured, reusable document, review it together for corrections, and request a format you can easily paste into future conversations.

This single artifact is what makes every subsequent 4D application (course design, materials creation) start from shared understanding instead of a blank slate.

## The 4Ds, applied to teaching

The 4D framework (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) maps onto teaching work directly:

- **Delegation** — before deciding *how* to collaborate with AI, get clear on *what* you're trying to accomplish and *why*. This is where you decide which parts of a task genuinely benefit from AI input and which need to stay entirely yours.
- **Description** — inviting AI into your specific problem space with rich context (your teaching context document, plus task-specific detail) rather than a bare request. Richer description produces answers that fit your actual students instead of a generic classroom.
- **Discernment** — evaluating every AI suggestion against your own expertise, and explicitly explaining *why* something works or doesn't for your students rather than just accepting or rejecting silently. Description and Discernment form a continuous feedback loop: each round of your explained reasoning refines the next round of AI output, and repeated cycles are what turn a generic tool into something closer to a thinking partner.
- **Diligence** — protecting student data and privacy, verifying factual accuracy, checking for bias, and maintaining transparency about where and how AI contributed to a piece of teaching material.

## Applying the 4Ds to course design

With a teaching context document in hand, course (re)design becomes a structured conversation rather than a cold start. The workflow has four stages:

1. **Plan your approach before involving AI** — which course or unit, what work actually needs doing (content selection, sequencing, objectives), and which parts require your judgment versus where AI can help.
2. **Initiate the collaboration richly** — share your teaching context document, explain the specific course and what "success" looks like for it, state your delegation thinking (what you want help with and why), invite clarifying questions, and share any real constraints up front.
3. **Work through content via the Description-Discernment loop** across three areas:
   - *Content identification* — ask AI to suggest topics/concepts from your context, then evaluate each against "does this serve YOUR students," explaining your reasoning back so the AI's next suggestions improve.
   - *Learning-journey mapping* — sequence a handful of sessions together, asking AI to take your students' perspective at transition points ("what might confuse students here," "what scaffolding is needed") and to explain how each session builds on the last.
   - *Learning objectives* — turn the content map into specific, measurable objectives, check alignment with institutional requirements, make sure they ladder up to course-level goals, and have AI help spot gaps or redundancies.
4. **Document and reflect** — verify accuracy and citations, record key decisions and *which* AI suggestions you rejected and why, and write a brief statement of AI's role in the planning process (useful both for your own records and for any institutional AI-use disclosure).

## Applying the 4Ds to learning materials and assignments

The same context and loop extend to the artifacts students actually see. A few patterns that recur across material types:

- **Lecture slide decks** — share course context plus the specific topic, your presentation style, and what slides should accomplish versus what you'll cover verbally; get an outline first, evaluate its logical flow and pacing before generating full content, then verify facts and note where citations or images are still needed.
- **Study/revision guides** — describe how your students actually study and what's worked before; have AI help identify must-master concepts, write self-check questions at varying difficulty, and anticipate common misconceptions; check that answer keys are accurate and aligned with your actual assessments.
- **In-class interactive exercises** — describe your classroom setup and timing constraints, get clear instructions and facilitation notes for yourself, and explicitly plan contingencies (what if the activity runs too fast or too slow).
- **Knowledge-check quizzes** — anchor every question to a specific learning objective, mix question types, ask AI to help write clean stems and plausible (not silly) distractors, and write explanations for *why* wrong answers are wrong, not just which answer is right. Verify every answer yourself before use.

**Academic integrity note:** the course is explicit that when students also have access to AI, the fix isn't avoiding AI in assessment design — it's using AI Fluency to design assessments that promote authentic learning in the first place (application, reasoning, and process-visible work rather than easily-generated recall answers).

**Final check across all material types:** verify factual accuracy, check for bias or confusing phrasing, consider accessibility and different learning styles, and document how AI contributed — the same Diligence habit that closes out course-design work applies here too.

## Quick reference

- Starting any AI collaboration on teaching → build the **teaching context document** first (values, constraints, students, methods); reuse it in every future conversation instead of re-explaining yourself.
- Redesigning a course or unit → work the four-stage flow: plan alone → describe richly → iterate content/journey/objectives through the Description-Discernment loop → document your reasoning and AI's role.
- Building slides, study guides, in-class activities, or quizzes → anchor each to your context and specific learning objectives, get a rough version first and evaluate before expanding, and always do a final accuracy/bias/accessibility pass yourself.
- Worried about students using AI to shortcut an assessment → redesign the assessment for authentic, process-visible learning rather than trying to out-detect AI use.
- Explaining AI's role to colleagues, students, or an institution → the Diligence habit of documenting what AI contributed and which suggestions you rejected (and why) is the same record you'd want for that disclosure.
- Want the full underlying framework rather than the teaching-specific application → see `ai-fluency-framework` for the general 4D foundations course this one builds on.
