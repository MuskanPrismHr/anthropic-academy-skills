## Purpose

Condensed reference to Anthropic Academy's "AI Fluency for Students" course, which applies the general AI Fluency Framework (4Ds) to being a learner. Pairs with `ai-fluency-framework` (general foundations) and `ai-fluency-for-educators` (the teaching-side application) — this is the student-side application layer. Pull the relevant section rather than repeating the whole thing.

## Why this matters, and the core distinction

The course's central move is distinguishing **using AI to do your work** from **using AI to help you learn**. Both are technically "using AI," but they produce opposite outcomes: the first outsources thinking and leaves you with a finished artifact and no new capability; the second keeps you doing the cognitive work while AI challenges, questions, and supports you. AI Fluency for a student means consistently choosing the second path even when the first is faster — because exams, interviews, and eventually a career will test what you actually know, not what you once had generated.

**Augmentation over automation** is the framing throughout: the goal isn't offloading effort, it's using AI to strengthen your own knowledge and critical thinking. A tutor/coach model beats an answer-giver model every time learning (not just task completion) is the actual objective.

## Step 1: Build a personal learning context document

Before using AI for any real academic work, build a **reusable context document** describing yourself as a learner, so every future conversation starts from shared understanding instead of a blank slate. Reflect first, then have a real conversation with an AI assistant to turn the reflection into a document.

What to reflect on before the conversation:
- **Academic context** — what you study, at what level, your strongest/weakest areas, typical assignment types, and your goals for the term and beyond.
- **Learning style and challenges** — how you learn best (visual, verbal, hands-on), what's hardest for you, where you most need support, and what motivates you to push through difficulty.
- **AI experience and goals** — your existing experience with AI tools, any concerns you have, what you hope AI can help you achieve, and what boundaries you want on AI use in your learning.

When building the document with AI: state clearly that the goal is enhancing learning, not replacing it; share your reflections and let the AI ask follow-up questions; be specific with real examples (mindful of privacy); and explicitly discuss your school's AI policy, when you want AI support versus independent work, and how you'll maintain academic integrity. Close by asking the AI to synthesize everything into a structured document, including a "How I Want to Work with AI" section, and save it to reuse at the start of future sessions.

## Step 2: AI as a learning partner, not a work-doer

Applying the 4Ds specifically to learning:
- **Delegation** — maintain problem awareness about what you're actually trying to learn, and choose AI systems/settings suited to education rather than defaulting to whatever's fastest.
- **Description** — steer the AI to act as a tutor or coach, not an answer-giver — this has to be stated explicitly, since a generic assistant will default to just answering.
- **Discernment** — honestly evaluate whether you're truly learning or just being carried along; this is the check that catches automation creeping in disguised as augmentation.
- **Diligence** — follow your institution's academic policies, and maintain the ability to explain and stand behind everything you submit, regardless of how much AI was involved in producing it.

**Configuring an AI study buddy:** share your learning context document (and, helpfully, the actual video transcripts if working through this course), then explicitly negotiate the AI's role — ask it to understand what you're trying to learn before helping, to ask you questions rather than hand over direct answers, to check your understanding before advancing, and to surface connections to things you already know. Useful per-task protocols to set up in advance: for problem-solving, "guide me with hints and questions, don't solve it for me"; for concept review, "test my understanding with progressively harder questions"; for exam prep, "quiz me and explain why wrong answers are wrong and right answers are right"; for writing, "help me develop my own arguments through questioning"; for reading comprehension, "ask me to explain key concepts in my own words."

**Building a living learning journal:** a separate, ongoing conversation (not a one-off) used for regular reflection — weekly is the recommended cadence. Each entry should cover what you worked on, what clicked versus what's still fuzzy, how AI specifically helped you learn (versus just complete something), what study strategies worked, and one concrete thing you need to review or practice more. Periodically add a monthly synthesis entry to spot patterns, and review the whole journal before exams to see your actual trajectory of understanding.

## Step 3: AI in career planning, without losing authenticity

The framing here: AI is a thinking partner for career development, not a decision-maker or a generic-content machine. Three areas it's most useful for: **career mapping** (AI supplies information and research; you supply self-knowledge and values — it can't decide what you should want), **skill building** (AI as a coach for developing specific capabilities, which requires active practice, not passive reading of AI output), and **job searching** (AI helps with research and rehearsal, but every representation of you has to stay genuinely yours — employers can spot generic AI-written application materials immediately, and it works against you).

**A concrete CV/resume improvement workflow:**
1. **Critique** — share your current CV/resume plus a specific target job description; ask the AI to analyze what the role actually requires (including unstated-but-relevant criteria) and how your CV/resume measures up against it.
2. **Information gathering** — let the AI ask you clarifying questions to surface relevant experience and achievements you haven't articulated well; answer honestly with specific detail.
3. **Revision** — work through the CV/resume section by section, strengthening the language around your real experience — every claim should be one you can back up, and the result should still feel like your own voice.

**Mock interview practice:** share your revised CV/resume and the job description, have the AI draft a handful of realistic interview questions, and insist it ask **one question at a time**, wait for your answer, ask at most one follow-up, then move on — this is the difference between a mock interview and just being handed a list of prep questions. Answer as you would live, with specific examples, and ask for a critique of content, clarity, style, and relevance at the end. The same one-question-at-a-time pattern adapts well to practicing cover letters or other communications.

**Guardrail worth repeating to yourself throughout:** you don't want to become an AI's (or anyone else's) version of you — every artifact you submit should be recognizably, defensibly yours.

## Step 4: Being the human in the loop

The course's closing idea: being "the human in the loop" means you retain the actual decision-making role in every AI interaction — what to ask, whether an output actually makes sense, and which values govern the interaction. This isn't a one-time setting, it's an ongoing posture to check yourself against.

**Drafting a personal AI-use policy** (a genuinely useful capstone artifact, not just an exercise) — go through this in two passes:
1. **Reflect offline first** on your core values around learning and integrity, your personal "red lines" where you will not use AI regardless of convenience, where AI support feels legitimately appropriate, and how you want to be perceived professionally. Widening the lens helps: consider how you'd want classmates, professors, or future employees to use AI too — it surfaces double standards in your own thinking.
2. **Draft the policy with AI**, covering at minimum: when you will and won't use AI (learning/practice situations versus exams and situations meant to demonstrate your own mastery), how you'll use it specifically for learning (tutor, not homework-completer; always able to explain what you submit), and a plan for keeping the policy something you'll actually reference — dated, specific, saved somewhere accessible, and revisited rather than written once and forgotten.

**Ongoing self-check questions** worth returning to periodically: Is this helping me grow? Am I developing the skills I actually need? Am I being honest with myself and others about AI's role in my work? The course's parting point is that your specific judgment, creativity, and experience remain the one thing genuinely irreplaceable in the loop — AI Fluency is a discipline you keep practicing, not a one-time skill you finish acquiring.

## Quick reference

- Starting any serious use of AI for schoolwork → build a **learning context document** first; reuse it instead of re-explaining yourself every conversation.
- Tempted to just get AI to produce the assignment → stop and ask which path you're on: is this AI doing the work, or AI helping you learn to do it? Only the second builds anything you'll still have on exam day.
- Setting up AI for regular studying → explicitly instruct it to act as tutor/coach (questions, hints, checks for understanding), not as an answer-dispenser — this needs to be stated, it won't happen by default.
- Want to actually track growth over time → a **weekly learning journal** entry, not just ad hoc AI chats you never revisit.
- Improving a CV/resume or cover letter → the critique → information-gathering → revision workflow, anchored to a real target job description, with every claim traceable to something real.
- Practicing for interviews → insist on one question at a time with a wait for your answer — a list of questions up front isn't a mock interview.
- Worried about where the line is between help and dishonesty → write an actual **personal AI-use policy** with explicit red lines, rather than deciding case by case under deadline pressure.
- Want the full underlying framework rather than the student-specific application → see `ai-fluency-framework` for the general 4D foundations course this one builds on.
