## Purpose

This is train-the-trainer material: how to teach the AI Fluency Framework's 4Ds (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) as curriculum content, not how to use AI to build your own lesson materials - that's `ai-fluency-for-educators`. Use this skill when the task is "design a course/workshop/unit that teaches people to work well with AI," where the 4Ds themselves are the subject being taught.

## Choosing a teaching sequence

The framework works as both a descriptive model (what happens when people work with AI) and a normative guide (how to do it well) - teaching it means covering both. Four sequencing approaches suit different contexts:

- Linear: teach Delegation → Description → Discernment → Diligence in order. Best for beginners who need sequential structure before they can handle complexity.
- Non-linear/flexible: start anywhere and move between competencies as the situation calls for it. Suits students who already have some AI experience and need to see the framework reflects real-world messiness.
- Focused: deep-dive a single competency rather than covering all four. Useful for a short session or when one competency (often Discernment or Diligence) is the actual gap.
- Two-loops: teach the framework as two nested processes (see below) rather than four discrete steps. Best when the goal is for students to internalize *how* the competencies interact, not just what each one is.

Pick based on time available and how much prior AI-collaboration experience students bring in, not by default to linear.

## The two loops

Splitting the 4Ds into two paired loops is a stronger teaching device than presenting four independent competencies, because it shows *why* they pair up:

**Delegation-Diligence loop** - the strategic, big-picture layer: deciding what to hand to AI and staying accountable for the outcome. This loop runs both directions - Diligence considerations (what am I accountable for, what needs transparency) should reshape Delegation choices, and Delegation choices determine what Diligence has to cover. Teach it through scenarios rather than checklists, so students see accountability as a constraint that clarifies and strengthens their choices rather than one that limits them.

**Description-Discernment loop** - the tactical, moment-to-moment layer: building a working "cognitive environment" with AI through iterative interaction, evaluated through three lenses - Product (is the output good), Process (is the collaborative method working), and Performance (is the AI's behavior appropriate to the goal). Students often arrive treating this as "prompt tricks"; the teaching move is redirecting them toward multi-turn assignments, modeling your own AI-collaboration process out loud, and having students document how their interactions evolve over time.

The two loops connect: Delegation-Diligence sets the strategic container, and Description-Discernment is the iterative work that fills it.

## Assessing the 4Ds

No single assessment type captures all four competencies - combine three approaches for a complete picture:

- Outcome assessment: evaluate the product created (what got automated well, what didn't).
- Process assessment: evaluate the journey - conversation logs, revision history, decision points.
- Reflection assessment: evaluate metacognitive awareness - can the student explain *why* they made the choices they made.

Each D calls for different evidence: Delegation shows up in goal-setting/task-breakdown/tool-selection choices, Description in conversation quality and iterative refinement, Discernment in critical evaluation of AI output, and Diligence in ethical/accountability reasoning. When building a rubric, write observable indicators at a few performance levels (e.g., emerging/developing/proficient) in your own course's language rather than generic terms - "good understanding" isn't assessable, "identifies which claims in the AI's output need verification and checks them" is.

## Designing assignments

Three assignment families map onto the three assessment types:

- Outcome-based: improve an AI output, compare outputs from different systems or prompts - reveals collaboration skill through the product.
- Process-based: annotated chat logs, recorded narration of a working session - makes otherwise-invisible decision-making visible.
- Reflection-based: learning journals, a personal AI-use policy statement - builds metacognitive awareness, but vary the format or students fatigue on journaling.

AI-Fluency assignments generate more artifacts to grade than a typical assignment (a chat log plus a final product plus a reflection, for instance). Manage the volume with detailed rubrics that let you grade quickly against clear indicators, heavier use of peer review, short "lightning round" conferences instead of written feedback on everything, and selective sampling rather than reading every artifact from every student in full.

## Mapping AI's impact onto your discipline

Disruption isn't uniform - AI might reshape assessment in a field while curriculum stays stable, or vice versa. Work through three questions for your own discipline: what's being automated well enough to change what you teach, where does human-AI partnership add the most value, and what do students need to stay accountable for in this field specifically. Treat some disruptions as opportunities to lean into and others as problems to actively guard against - your disciplinary judgment is what tells them apart, and AI Fluency is meant to amplify that expertise, not replace it.

Making tacit disciplinary knowledge explicit is the concrete mechanism: define what "quality" means in your field for Discernment, map your field's communication norms for Description, clarify how work actually decomposes into delegable pieces for Delegation, and codify your field's ethical standards for Diligence. This is easier done with colleagues than alone - a shared understanding of each D in your discipline produces a stronger, more consistent framework than one instructor's individual take.

## Quick reference

- Beginners with no AI-collaboration background → teach the 4Ds linearly.
- Students with some AI experience, or a short session on one gap → non-linear or focused approach.
- Want students to internalize *why* the competencies interact, not just memorize four labels → teach via the two loops.
- Grading only the final product → you're missing Description/Discernment/Diligence evidence; add a process or reflection artifact.
- Rubric language reads as vague ("good understanding," "uses AI effectively") → rewrite each indicator as an observable, subject-specific action.
- Assignment generates too many artifacts to grade in full → add a detailed rubric, shift to peer review, or sample rather than reading everything.
- Deciding what to change in your course because of AI → separate what's actually being automated from where partnership still adds the most value, using your own disciplinary judgment as the filter.
