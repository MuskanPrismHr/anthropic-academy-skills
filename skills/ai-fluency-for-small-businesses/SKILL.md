## Purpose

Small business owners face a version of AI fluency shaped by two constraints most enterprise advice ignores: no one else is checking your work, and every task you hand to AI still has your name and your customers attached to it. This skill applies the 4D framework (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) to that reality - the outer Delegation-Diligence loop for the big call of whether and how to use AI on something, the inner Description-Discernment loop for getting a good result once you've decided to.

## Building your business-context document

Before using AI for anything substantive, build a reusable summary of your business so you're not re-explaining yourself in every conversation. Have a back-and-forth with AI covering your mission and values, your products/services and customers, the scope of your role and any team members' roles, your recurring challenges (cash flow, staffing, competition, time), your industry and local market constraints, and your goals for AI. Ask AI to synthesize the conversation into a structured document you can paste into future conversations - your "AI briefing sheet." Be mindful of sensitive business data even at this stage; the safe-sharing guidance below applies here too.

## Testing AI before you trust it

Don't take a tool's reliability on faith - test it against something you can already verify. Pick a topic in your own domain where you'd immediately spot an error (your margin calculations, local zoning rules, licensing requirements), then run three checks: a versatility check (ask it to explain the same thing three ways - to a customer, a partner, a new hire - and see if the substance actually shifts or just the vocabulary), a hallucination check (ask for specific resources - a trade association, a supplier, a regulatory body - then verify at least one actually exists), and a knowledge-cutoff check (ask something time-sensitive or local and see whether it flags uncertainty or presents stale information as current). What you find sets your baseline for how much independent verification a given kind of task needs going forward.

## Description-Discernment for research: policy, market, and competitive intelligence

This is where fabrication and staleness cost real money, so the loop needs to be deliberate. On the Description side, give AI your business's core offering, your size/geography/customer profile, and the exact question you're answering - not "analyze the market" but "is there demand for a subscription version of my product in my area." On the Discernment side, treat every specific claim as unverified until checked: confirm any competitor or organization named actually exists, verify exact figures (fees, deadlines, market-size numbers, pricing benchmarks) against a primary source, and watch for information that's simply gone stale (a business that's closed, a regulation that's since changed). Loop back with a sharper prompt based on what the first pass got wrong or missed - the first response is a starting point, not an answer.

## Sharing business data safely

Before sharing any sales, customer, or operational data with AI, check what tier of tool you're using - a free consumer product and a paid account with a data-retention agreement are not interchangeable, and the more sensitive the data, the more that distinction matters. Then ask whether the goal actually requires identifying details at all - for most pattern-level analysis (why did repeat bookings drop, what do reviews cluster around) it doesn't. Replace customer names with generic identifiers, drop exact contact information, and consider whether dollar figures need to be exact or could be ranges. Before opening AI, write your own brief first: what you're trying to accomplish, what you already suspect from having looked at the data yourself, and what a useful output would look like - this is what lets you actually judge what AI hands back rather than accepting it uncritically. If something does go wrong with a shared conversation, act fast: delete it and request data deletion from the provider.

## Building a workflow automation: Description × Diligence

This is the highest-leverage application in the framework, because it turns a one-off task into something repeatable. Start with Problem Awareness - what do customers actually ask repeatedly, and which of those patterns are genuinely standardized versus requiring judgment. Sort candidate tasks into three tiers: AI can handle outright (documented FAQs, standard policies), AI can assist but a human decides (drafts of follow-up emails, feedback summaries), and human should handle regardless (complaints, negotiations, anything emotionally sensitive or high-stakes).

Once you've picked a task, describe it on three axes:

- **Product** - the end result, the inputs it receives, and the outputs it should produce.
- **Process** - the step-by-step logic, written like instructions for a capable but extremely literal new employee: what happens first, what the decision points are, when it should escalate to a human, and what reference material it needs (your FAQ page, price list, policies).
- **Performance** - the tone, how it should handle uncertainty ("say so and flag for review - never guess"), and explicit boundaries on what it should never do (never promise a specific time, never share one customer's information with another, never offer an unapproved discount).

Then plan Diligence before deploying, not after: Creation Diligence asks why this task is actually a good fit and what could go wrong (list at least three failure modes and a safeguard for each); Deployment Diligence decides your review cadence (review everything at first, with a defined bar for when you'd ease off) and what would make you pause the automation entirely; Transparency Diligence decides who needs to know AI is involved, the actual disclosure language you'll use, and how someone can reach a real person. Test against 3-5 real past examples before trusting it, and expect two or three rounds of refinement - a single pass rarely gets the descriptions right.

## Avoiding AI dependency and writing a team policy

Being "the human in the loop" means deciding what problems AI should solve, not just supervising it after the fact. The practical check against creeping dependency is simple: can you still explain what the AI is doing in a given process? If yes, that's healthy augmentation. If not, that's the signal to rework the process until someone can explain it end to end. Set norms early about what happens to the time AI saves - efficiency gains that don't translate into either better customer service or your own bandwidth back tend to just get absorbed and forgotten.

A one-page AI policy documents this for yourself and your team in three sections: what we use AI for (specific approved tasks and tools), what stays human (the work AI doesn't touch, and why), and how we stay accountable (oversight, transparency, and what happens when something goes wrong). Draft it by sharing your context document and your honest answers to those three questions with AI, then stress-test the result - read it as if a customer had just asked "do you use AI in your business?" and check whether it gives a clear, honest answer.

## Quick reference

- Starting to use AI for your business at all → build a context document first so you're not re-explaining your business every time.
- Deciding how much to trust a tool → test it first against a topic you know well (versatility, hallucination, and knowledge-cutoff checks), not on faith.
- Researching regulations, competitors, or market data → give tight Description (your business specifics, the exact question); verify every specific claim (existence, figures, currency) before acting on it.
- About to share sales/customer data → check the tool's data tier, strip identifying details unless the goal truly requires them, and write your own brief before you open AI so you can actually judge what comes back.
- Building a recurring automation → describe it on Product/Process/Performance, then plan Creation/Deployment/Transparency Diligence before deploying, and test on real examples first.
- Worried about AI dependency creeping in → the test is whether you can still explain what it's doing; if not, rework the process rather than avoiding AI altogether.
- Setting rules for your team or customers → write a one-page policy covering what AI is used for, what stays human, and how you stay accountable, then stress-test it against a direct customer question.
